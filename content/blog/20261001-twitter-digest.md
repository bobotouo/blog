> You have been throwing away electricity your entire life. Not a little. Roughly 3 joules, every single step, since the day you learned to walk. Millions of steps. Gone into the pavement as heat and noise and nothing. The floor took it. The floor never said thank you. The floor never paid you a cent. Someone finally noticed. And what they built with that "waste" is more interesting, more uncomfortable, and far more profitable than the headline you think you're about to read. Stay with me. This is a long one, and every section earns the next. --- 1. THE ENERGY YOU NEVER SAW LEAVING Stand up. Take one step. For a fraction of a second, your body weight, somewhere between 600 and 900 newtons of force, presses down through your heel onto the ground. Your muscles paid for that motion with real calories. Those calories came from real food. That food came from real sunlight, real soil, real diesel, real fertilizer, real water. And what happened to all that work at the moment your foot landed? It turned into a tiny thud and a tiny puff of warmth. That is the entire return on investment. Now multiply. The average person takes somewhere around 5,000 to 7,000 steps a day. Over a lifetime, that's well over 100 million steps. Each one a small, honest, perfectly good packet of mechanical energy, spent and abandoned. Physics has a name for what happened to it: dissipation. Engineers have a nicer name for the opportunity: harvestable kinetic energy. The uncomfortable part is not that the energy is wasted. Everything wastes energy. Your car wastes about 70 percent of the fuel it burns. Your laptop charger warms your desk for free. Wasted energy is the default state of the universe. The uncomfortable part is that we designed the most walked-on surfaces on Earth, train stations, airport corridors, stadium tunnels, shopping streets, crosswalks, as if the energy of a billion footsteps was not worth a single engineer's afternoon. Then the afternoon happened. --- 2. THE NUMBER THAT SHOULD HUMBLE EVERY HYPE MERCHANT Before anyone gets carried away, let's do the math nobody puts in the promo reel. One step, captured well, gives you on the order of 2 to 5 joules of usable electrical energy. Let's be generous and say 4. One kilowatt-hour, the thing your utility bills you for, is 3,600,000 joules. So to earn ONE kilowatt-hour, you need 900,000 footsteps. A modern smartphone battery holds roughly 15 watt-hours, or 54,000 joules. To fully charge it from footsteps alone, you'd need about 13,500 steps landing on generating tiles. That's a full day of walking for one person. To charge one phone. Once. If you just felt a small pang of disappointment, good. Hold onto it. The pang is the most important thing in this post, because the entire industry around this technology has spent a decade either hiding from that number or building a smarter answer to it. We'll get to the smarter answer. But first, let me make the number even more brutal. There are about 8 billion humans. Assume everyone walks 5,000 steps a day. That's 40 trillion steps daily. At 4 joules each, that's 160 trillion joules per day, roughly 44 gigawatt-hours. Sounds gigantic, right? The planet consumes something like 29,000 terawatt-hours of electricity per year. Our fantasy scenario, in which EVERY step by EVERY human on EVERY surface is captured with perfect equipment, produces about 16 terawatt-hours per year. That is roughly 0.05 percent of global electricity demand. Not solar. Not wind. Not a replacement for anything. A rounding error with legs. So if the story ended here, this would be a tale about a clever gadget that could never matter. It does not end here. --- 3. WHY THE OBVIOUS PITCH IS THE WRONG PITCH Every time this technology appears online, the same comment shows up within four minutes: "Great, but it will never power a city." Correct. And also completely irrelevant. Here is the mistake that almost everyone makes. They benchmark a footstep against a power plant. That is like benchmarking a match against a wildfire and concluding matches are useless. Matches are not for heating cities. Matches start things. A footstep is not a power source. A footstep is a signal that happens to come with a small battery attached. Read that again slowly. The moment you accept that the electricity is not the product, the whole field flips upside down. You stop asking "how many watts?" and you start asking "what can be powered by a few joules, and what is the information worth that this footstep just handed me?" Turns out both answers are wild. --- 4. HOW YOU TURN A STOMP INTO ELECTRICITY There are only a few honest ways to convert a footstep into current, and each one is a different engineering religion. PIEZOELECTRIC. Certain crystals and ceramics produce a voltage when squeezed. Press them, get a tiny spike of charge. No moving parts, silent, compact. The catch: the spike is small and brief, and crystals fatigue after millions of hits. Beautiful in a lab, brutal on a busy sidewalk. ELECTROMAGNETIC. The floor tile drops a few millimeters under your weight. That downward motion is converted into rotation, and the rotation spins a small generator. This is the same principle as the dynamo on an old bicycle, shrunk and hidden under a slab. More energy per step. More mechanical complexity. More things that can wear out. TRIBOELECTRIC. Two materials touch, separate, and swap electrons, the same effect that zaps your fingers on a doorknob in winter. Researchers have turned it into nanogenerators thin enough to sit in a shoe sole. Wildly promising, still fighting durability and scale. Three routes. One brutal design constraint: a floor must feel like a floor. If the tile sinks too far, people feel like they are walking on a mattress and stop trusting it. If it sinks too little, you harvest almost nothing. Engineers live in the narrow band between "invisible" and "useful," and that band is only a few millimeters wide. Now add reality. The tile has to survive rain, snow, salt, spilled coffee, stiletto heels, delivery carts, marathon crowds, and the occasional drunk person doing a victory dance. It must last years. It must be safe. It must be cheap enough to justify itself. Every one of those requirements fights every other one. That's why this took twenty years instead of two. --- 5. THE PART THE PROMO VIDEOS DON'T SAY OUT LOUD Here is the quiet truth the industry learned the hard way. You cannot sell a few joules to the grid. Nobody will pay for it. The payback period on the hardware, measured purely in electricity, is longer than the lifespan of the hardware itself. So the electricity-only business model is dead on arrival. Then a much sharper question appeared: what else is a step? A step is an event. It has a timestamp. It has a location. It has a force signature. Put a few hundred of those tiles across a plaza and suddenly you are not looking at a power project. You are looking at a sensor network that does not need a wire, does not need a battery swap, and does not need anyone's permission. Think about what a building owner, a city planner, a retailer, a stadium operator or an event organizer would pay to know: How many people actually crossed this exact spot, this hour, this week? Which corridor is overloaded at 5:40 pm? Where do crowds slow down, bunch up, or turn around? Which entrance is dead, and which one is secretly carrying the whole building? Did that expensive installation change how people move, yes or no? Cameras can answer some of this, but cameras come with cost, blind spots, lighting problems, and a privacy debate nobody wants. A floor that counts footfall, with no faces and no identity, is a different kind of instrument. This is the pivot that quietly reshaped the category. The electricity became the excuse. The data became the business. Read that as a product lesson, not just a tech one: when the headline feature cannot pay the bills, look for the by-product that can. --- 6. THE OTHER USE NOBODY EXPECTS: POWER SO SMALL IT SOUNDS FAKE Now flip the earlier math on its head. A few joules is useless to a city. But a few joules is a LOT to certain electronics. A modern low-power sensor can wake up, measure something, send a short wireless message, and go back to sleep on a tiny fraction of a joule. An LED, a display segment, a small transmitter, a counter: these live comfortably in the microjoule-to-millijoule world. Which means one footstep can, in principle, light a small display, ping a sensor, or keep a wireless node alive for a while. This is the real engineering frontier, and it has a name: energy harvesting. The dream is a world of devices that never need a battery change, never need a charging cable, and never end up in a landfill after two years. Think about the scale of that problem. Analysts have long talked about tens of billions of connected devices. Every one of them needs power. Every battery is a tiny chemical liability with a shelf life and a disposal problem. Swapping batteries in sensors buried in floors, walls, bridges, tunnels and factory lines is a logistics nightmare that grows with every device we add. A sensor that eats its power from its own surroundings is not a gadget. It is the removal of an entire maintenance industry. And a footstep is one of the richest, most predictable, most abundant motions in any human environment. --- 7. THE GRAVEYARD OF GOOD IDEAS Let's be adults about this. Not every attempt at squeezing power from human motion worked. Some nightclub floors, dance halls and train station experiments over the years produced more press coverage than electricity. Some pilot projects ran for a season, powered a few lights, and quietly disappeared when funding, maintenance or interest ran out. Skeptical engineers have written entire critiques pointing out that the energy per step is tiny, the hardware is expensive, and the same money spent on a few solar panels would out-produce the floor by orders of magnitude. They are not wrong. If your only goal is kilowatt-hours per dollar, a rooftop solar panel beats a footstep floor so badly it is almost unfair. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. But look at what that critique actually proves. It proves that footstep energy loses the raw-power contest. It does not prove that footsteps are worthless. It proves that they are the wrong tool for one job, and possibly an excellent tool for a completely different one. That difference, between "bad at the obvious use" and "great at a hidden use," is where nearly every interesting technology lives for its first ten years. --- 8. THE WORLD IS FULL OF THROWN-AWAY MOTION Once you see the footstep, you cannot unsee the rest. Look at everything else we let fall on the floor. TRAINS. A moving train carries enormous kinetic energy. For a century, braking meant turning that energy into heat inside brake pads. Modern electric trains do something smarter: the motors run backward as generators, and the braking energy flows back into the overhead line or onboard storage so another train can use it. Whole metro systems now recover a meaningful share of their traction energy this way. CARS. Every hybrid and electric vehicle does the same trick. Lift off the accelerator and the car starts refilling its own battery. It is so normal now that drivers forget it was once considered science fiction. ELEVATORS. In tall buildings, a heavy elevator car descending with a full load is basically a falling weight. Modern drives capture that descent and feed the electricity back to the building instead of burning it off in resistors. DATA CENTERS AND FACTORIES. Waste heat that used to be vented into the sky is now piped into district heating networks in several cities, warming apartments with the leftovers of computation. Notice the pattern. Every single one of these started as "too small to matter," "too complicated to bother," or "not worth the engineering." Every single one became standard once someone did the unglamorous work of making it cheap, durable and invisible. The footstep is simply the last, smallest, most human entry on that list. And it is the only one where the source of the energy is you. --- 9. THE PSYCHOLOGY TRICK HIDING INSIDE THE HARDWARE Here is where it stops being about engineering and starts being about people. Ask a hundred strangers whether they would walk out of their way to save two watt-hours of electricity. Almost no one will. Now ask them whether they would walk across a floor that lights up under their feet. Watch what happens. Kids run back and forth. Adults pretend not to care and then take one more step. Groups start racing. Someone films it. Someone asks, "Wait, is that from me?" That reaction is the most valuable output of the entire system, and it does not appear on any energy meter. Behavioral scientists have a name for the underlying mechanism: immediate, visible feedback. When an action produces an instant, tangible response, humans repeat it. Fitness trackers proved it. Smart meters proved it. Even a simple light that turns green when you do the right thing changes behavior. Most sustainability messaging fails because it is abstract. "Save the planet" is a slogan. "Your foot just made that light glow" is an experience. One of those gets shared, and the other gets scrolled past. So the floor does something no poster, no policy and no lecture can do: it makes an invisible physical fact feel personal, in about half a second. That is not a gimmick. That is a communication channel disguised as a piece of pavement. --- 10. WHAT A SMART CITY ACTUALLY NEEDS Every "smart city" pitch of the last decade has promised the same thing: sensors everywhere, data everywhere, optimization everywhere. And every city planner who has tried to deploy it has hit the same wall: power and maintenance. Running cables to thousands of measurement points is expensive. Trenching a street costs a fortune and angers every driver for months. Replacing batteries in ten thousand sensors is a job that never ends. Solar works on rooftops and poles, but not under a covered walkway, inside a station, or along a tunnel wall. So the smart city keeps arriving in slide decks and keeps stalling in real life. Now imagine a surface that measures its own use, transmits it wirelessly, and powers itself from the traffic it measures. The busier the place gets, the more energy the system has to work with, and the more data it has to report. Demand and supply rise together. That is a rare property. Most infrastructure gets more strained under load. This kind gets more capable. Self-scaling infrastructure. Keep that phrase. It will matter again. --- 11. THE FIVE LESSONS HIDDEN IN A STEP If you build things, write things, sell things or invest in things, here is what a tile under a shoe quietly teaches. LESSON ONE: Judge a technology by its best use, not its most obvious one. Footsteps lose as a power plant and win as a sensor. Ask what a thing is uniquely good at before you judge it against what it is famously bad at. LESSON TWO: When the main feature cannot pay for itself, follow the by-product. The electricity was the story. The data was the revenue. Half of all great business models were originally the exhaust of something else. LESSON THREE: Feelings move faster than facts. A number about kilowatt-hours changes no one. A floor that glows under your feet changes minds in a single afternoon. If you want people to care, let them touch it. LESSON FOUR: Durability is the whole game. Anyone can build a demo that works ten times. Almost nobody can build a product that works ten million times in the rain. The boring engineering is the moat. LESSON FIVE: Small and constant beats big and rare. A trickle that never stops can outperform a flood that arrives once. Most compounding, in energy, in money, in skill, looks unimpressive on any single day. --- 12. THE OBJECTIONS, ANSWERED HONESTLY Let me argue against myself, because a post that only flatters its subject is an advertisement. "It's a gimmick." Sometimes, yes. A floor installed purely as a photo opportunity, with no data strategy and no maintenance plan, is a gimmick. The technology is real. The deployments vary wildly in seriousness. "Solar is thousands of times better." For raw electricity, true. But solar cannot count people, cannot react to a step, and cannot run under a roof. Different jobs, different tools. "The payback never works." On electricity alone, correct. It only works when the value comes from data, engagement, branding, safety lighting, or research. Anyone selling it on kilowatt-hours is selling a fantasy. "Wear and tear will kill it." This is the strongest objection and the one that decides winners. Every year of real-world durability is worth more than every press release. "What about privacy?" A floor sensor that registers pressure and timing, not faces, is arguably one of the more privacy-friendly ways to measure crowds. But the moment data is combined with other sources, the usual rules apply: collect the minimum, keep it anonymous, say clearly what you do with it. Notice what happened there. The technology did not get defended. It got narrowed. It stopped being a miracle and became a tool with a job description. Tools with job descriptions are the ones that survive. --- 13. WHAT TO WATCH IN THE NEXT FIVE YEARS If you want to know whether this category becomes real infrastructure or stays a curiosity, ignore the demos and watch these signals. SIGNAL ONE: Are cities buying it for measurement, not for spectacle? The day a transport authority or a stadium operator budgets it as a line item next to cameras and counters, the category is real. SIGNAL TWO: Do the maintenance numbers get published? Real infrastructure companies eventually talk about failure rates, replacement cycles and cost per year. Marketing companies never do. SIGNAL THREE: Does the harvesting hardware shrink? Every improvement in low-power chips lowers the energy a sensor needs. Every improvement in harvesters raises the energy a step yields. The two curves are heading toward each other, and the crossing point is where battery-free devices become boring and normal. SIGNAL FOUR: Do materials break the compromise? Better piezo ceramics, better triboelectric films and better bearings could make a tile thinner, quieter, more durable and cheaper, all at once. That is the type of quiet breakthrough that changes an industry without a single headline. SIGNAL FIVE: Does it leave the plaza? The moment this shows up in schools, gyms, hospital corridors, factory walkways, shoe soles and stadium tunnels as a default feature, not a showpiece, the transition is done. --- 14. THE BIGGER IDEA UNDER THE FLOOR Zoom out one more level. For most of the industrial age, energy was something you dug up, burned, shipped, and delivered to a passive customer at the end of a wire. The energy story of the next fifty years is different. It is about noticing that energy is already everywhere, in vibration, heat, light, motion, pressure, radio waves, in every gap between what a system takes in and what it gives off, and that tiny amounts are enough if the devices asking for them are frugal enough. A world of a trillion small sensors cannot be powered by wires and batteries. It has to be powered by ambient leftovers. Footsteps, machine hum, temperature differences, sunlight through a window, the vibration of a passing truck. Think about that as a mindset, not just a technology. The winners of the next era will not only be the people who generate more. They will be the people who waste less, notice more, and turn the invisible surplus of everyday life into something usable. Every system leaks. The question is who builds the bucket. --- 15. THE UNCOMFORTABLE MIRROR Now the part that has nothing to do with tiles. You, too, leak. Your attention leaks into feeds that give nothing back. Your hours leak into meetings that could have been messages. Your money leaks into subscriptions you forgot. Your energy leaks into arguments you will not remember by Thursday. Your best ideas leak because you never wrote them down. Every day, the average person burns huge amounts of effort and captures almost none of it. Not because the effort was bad. Because nothing was built to catch it. The lesson of a footstep floor is not "how clever." The lesson is: what you do every day already carries value, and most of it hits the ground unclaimed. The engineers did not invent new energy. They simply stopped ignoring the old energy. The difference between wasting and harvesting is often just a system, a small one, placed at exactly the spot where the effort lands. Ask yourself: where does my effort land? And what is under it? --- 16. ONE MORE NUMBER BEFORE THE SUMMARY Ten thousand people crossing one busy corridor on an ordinary weekday is not unusual. Not for a station. Not for a mall entrance. Not for a stadium gate on match day. Ten thousand steps on a good tile is around 40,000 joules, enough to run a small low-power sensor for a very long time, or to keep a modest display glowing through an entire evening. Zero cables. Zero battery swaps. Zero invoices from the grid. The crowd is the power plant, and the crowd is also the customer. --- 17. THE SHORT VERSION, FOR THE SCROLLERS WHO SKIPPED TO THE BOTTOM A footstep is worth about 3 joules. That will never power a city, and it was never supposed to. The electricity is the hook. The data is the business. The feeling under your shoe is the marketing. The battery-free sensor is the future. Solar wins on watts. Footsteps win on where the watts happen to be: inside stations, under roofs, along corridors, at the exact spot where people actually are. Every surface humans walk on is a sensor waiting to be switched on. Most are still waiting for someone to notice. --- 18. THE PREDICTION Here is mine, and you are free to screenshot it and mock me in ten years. Nobody will ever celebrate the day a footstep floor "solved energy." That day will not come. Instead, one morning you will walk through a station, a school or a stadium tunnel, and something will quietly count you, light your way, and report the crowd, without a wire, without a battery, without a headline. You will not even look down. That is how real technology arrives. Not with a parade. With a Tuesday. And the quiet, unremarkable Tuesday will have been built by people who refused to look at a wasted footstep and shrug. --- 19. YOUR MOVE If this post made you look at the ground differently for even three seconds, it did its job. Now do one thing. Pick one place where your own effort lands and disappears. One habit, one workflow, one repeated action that produces nothing you can point at. Put a tiny system under it. A note. A counter. A template. A rule. Anything that catches a few joules. Then watch what a small, constant trickle does over a year. Not a dramatic transformation. Just a quiet, compounding difference that nobody else can see yet, and that you will not be able to stop noticing. If you build hardware, look at the surfaces in your industry that people touch, walk on, lean on, open, close or sit on. Each one is a sensor and a generator that nobody has switched on yet. If you invest, stop asking "how many watts" and start asking "what does the by-product pay?" If you are simply curious, keep noticing. Most breakthroughs begin as somebody refusing to accept that a thing is just waste. --- Follow for more posts that turn small physical facts into big ideas.
