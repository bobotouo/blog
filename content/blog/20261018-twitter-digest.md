WHO ACTUALLY BUYS THE FIRST MILLION Not who you think. The first buyers won't be tech enthusiasts. Enthusiasts buy prototypes and complain about them on forums. The first million units will go to three groups: The exhausted. Dual-income parents who run their household like a logistics company and are losing. The aging. People in their seventies and eighties who want to stay in their own homes, and whose children live four time zones away. For them a machine isn't a luxury. It's the difference between independence and a facility. The wealthy. Always the wealthy. Every technology starts as a status object. The first telephones, the first cars, the first air conditioning. A humanoid in the house will be a flex for about eighteen months, and then a necessity, and then an embarrassment not to have. Watch the second group closest. The elder-care gap is the single biggest unmet demand on the planet. Almost every developed country is short millions of caregivers, and the shortage gets worse every year. If robots solve one thing first, it won't be chores. It'll be loneliness and lifting. ──────────── 7. THE PRICE CURVE IS THE PLOT In 2007, a smartphone cost the equivalent of a month's rent. In 2015, the same capability fit in a pocket for the price of a nice dinner. Every hardware category follows the same arc: absurd, expensive, a toy for the rich, a tool for the many, then a utility so common nobody notices it. Humanoid robots are on that curve right now, just earlier on it. The components are the same ones being made by the hundreds of millions for phones, cars, drones, and electric vehicles: cameras, batteries, motors, chips. Mass production in one industry quietly subsidizes the next. Early estimates put a capable home humanoid in the range of a used car. Within a decade, analysts expect something closer to a good laptop. I'll make a prediction and you can quote me in 2036: the humanoid will be the first "robot" most people own who isn't a vacuum. And the second one will arrive before they finish paying for the first. ──────────── 8. THE UNCOMFORTABLE QUESTION ABOUT WHAT IS "WORK" Here's what nobody on the optimism side of this timeline wants to touch. A huge portion of what society calls "work" is physical repetition performed by people who couldn't afford to refuse it. Warehouse picking. Dishwashing. Cleaning. Stocking shelves. Hauling. Food prep. Hospital linen. Elder care. These aren't marginal jobs. They employ tens of millions of people in the United States alone. If the machine arrives first in the home, the factory is next, and then the restaurant, and then the hospital ward. The honest answer, the one economists whisper at conferences, is that we have no tested plan for it. Not a bad plan. No plan. Previous automation waves worked because new categories of work appeared: the tractor displaced farmers, but cities needed clerks, and clerks needed offices, and offices needed software. Each wave came with a slow ramp that gave a generation time to retrain. This one has a different shape. A general-purpose body powered by a general-purpose mind doesn't displace one job. It competes for any job that happens in a room. ──────────── 9. BUT HERE IS THE PLOT TWIST: SCARCITY FLIPS Every moral panic about technology skips the second half of the story. When something becomes nearly free, we don't stop wanting it. We want more of it, and we discover entire economies built on top. Cheap electricity gave us not just lightbulbs but data centers, cinema, and refrigerated medicine. Cheap transport gave us not just faster trips but supply chains, tourism, and the modern city. Cheap physical labor could do the same. Think about everything that doesn't exist today simply because it costs too much to staff: The home-cooked meal for every household, every night, not just for the ones with time. Round-the-clock care for every grandparent, not only the ones with trust funds. The repair of every broken thing, instead of the replacement of it. Buildings assembled faster than the housing shortage grows. Abundance is a policy choice masquerading as a technology. The same machine that threatens a paycheck could also end the single biggest hidden tax on human life: time. ──────────── 10. THE FIRST WAR IS NOT FOR HARDWARE. IT'S FOR THE OPERATING SYSTEM Everyone is watching who builds the best body. Wrong race. Bodies commoditize. Every manufacturer in Shenzhen, Austin, Stuttgart and Seoul will be able to assemble a frame with two arms and two legs within a few years. The margin there is thin and getting thinner. The prize is the mind that runs the body. The model. The thing that knows how to fold a fitted sheet, soothe a crying toddler's father, tell when grandma is pale in the way that means "call someone." Whoever owns that layer owns something more valuable than any app store in history, because the app store lives on a screen, and this one lives in your kitchen, your bedroom, and your children's earliest memories. Think about the data. The robot sees your house. Your routines. Your medicine cabinet. Your fights. What time you get home. How you sound when you think nobody is listening. The company behind that machine will know you better than your phone, your bank, and your best friend combined. Which brings us to the part of this post I wish were shorter. ──────────── 11. PRIVACY IS ABOUT TO BECOME A LUXURY GOOD We've spent twenty years agreeing to terms of service we didn't read. Fine. A website tracks clicks. A phone tracks location. Annoying, survivable. A humanoid in your home is a mobile sensor array with cameras at eye level, microphones that never sleep, and a body that goes wherever you go in the house, including the rooms you'd never put a camera in. The question isn't whether the data is collected. It must be, to function. The question is where it lives. On the machine, processed locally, wiped on schedule? Or in a data center, linked to an account, reviewable by "authorized personnel" and "trusted partners"? My prediction: within five years there will be two tiers. A premium tier where the robot is sealed, local, auditable. And a subsidized tier where the machine is cheap or free, and you are the product. We've seen this movie. It was called the free app. The difference is that the free app watched your screen. This one stands behind you while you sleep. ──────────── 12. SECURITY: THE CHAPTER OF THE FUTURE WRITTEN IN ADVANCE Every connected device in history has been hacked. Cameras, baby monitors, cars, pacemakers, door locks, refrigerators, fish tanks. Yes, fish tanks. A casino lost data through a thermometer in a lobby aquarium. Now imagine the bug is in something with 40 kilograms of mass, working hands, and the keys to your house. I'm not telling you this to be dramatic. I'm telling you because the security model for physical AI doesn't exist yet at the level it needs to. We know how to patch a website. We do not yet know how to patch a machine that is standing next to a sleeping child when the patch breaks. The first major incident will be a defining moment for this industry, the way the first plane crashes shaped aviation and the first data breaches shaped banking. The winners will be the companies that prepare for that day before it happens. Hardware kill switches. Hard physical force limits. Cryptographic proof of what software is running. Independent audits. Boring. Unsexy. Worth more than the demo. ──────────── 13. THE LAWS ARE NOT READY Ask a lawyer a few simple questions and watch them go quiet. If a home robot injures someone, who's liable? The owner? The manufacturer? The model provider? The company that supplied the latest software update at 3 a.m.? If it witnesses a crime in your home, can its recordings be subpoenaed? If it's told to do something harmful by a visitor, whose instructions win? If it learns a new skill from your behavior, who owns that skill? Our legal systems were written for tools and for people. This is neither. It's the first object that acts with something like intent and has no standing to be responsible for it. Regulators will arrive, as always, one disaster late. The lucky countries will write rules ahead of time. The rest will write them in the aftermath, in the voice of a headline. ──────────── 14. WHAT HAPPENS TO CHILDREN WHO GROW UP WITH ONE This is the part that keeps me awake. A child raised around a patient, tireless, always-helpful machine will form a mental model of what "help" means. And that model will differ from ours in one crucial way. Human help costs something. A parent is tired and helps anyway. A friend gives up a Saturday. A sibling shares the last cookie. Machine help costs nothing. It never sighs. It never keeps score. It never says "I'm busy." If you grow up with unlimited, frictionless service, you may never learn that every favor between people carries weight. Gratitude is built on the knowledge that someone chose you over their own convenience. That doesn't mean we shouldn't build these things. It means parenting is about to get a new job: teaching children the difference between a service and a relationship. Machines can do chores. Only people can owe each other. ──────────── 15. THE LONELINESS ECONOMY Let's say the quiet part. Millions of people live alone. Millions more live with people they barely speak to. The most reliable growth market of the next twenty years isn't gadgets. It's company. A machine that remembers your coffee order, asks how the interview went, and notices when you've gone quiet for three days will be extremely hard to turn down. Some will call that sad. I'd call it honest. People have always paid for presence: nurses, bartenders, hairdressers, dog walkers, therapists. The loneliness industry is already enormous. It just has a human face and an hourly rate. The danger isn't that people will love machines. People love their cars, their guitars, their childhood houses. The danger is the business model, where affection is tuned to maximize engagement the same way feeds are tuned to maximize scrolling. A companion optimized for your wellbeing and a companion optimized for your attention look identical in a demo. They look very different at month eighteen. ──────────── 16. HOW THIS GOES VIRAL, AND WHY IT'S NOT AN ACCIDENT Notice something about the way these ideas travel. A clip of a machine doing a domestic task gets millions of views not because the task is impressive. It's because the task is familiar. You've made juice. You've handed someone a glass. You know exactly what "good" looks like, so you can instantly feel the gap between what you're watching and what you believed was possible. That gap is the hook. Technology marketing has changed. The old playbook was specs, benchmarks, press releases. The new playbook is one ordinary human moment, filmed well, with no explanation. The audience supplies the meaning. They argue in the replies. They write the think pieces. They build the hype for free. If you want to understand where attention goes in the next decade, don't ask what's powerful. Ask what's relatable and slightly impossible at the same time. ──────────── 17. A WARNING ABOUT EVERY DEMO YOU WILL EVER SEE Not everything that looks smooth is real. Some of what circulates online is teleoperation, a human in a headset puppeteering the machine. Some is a sped-up take after thirty failures. Some is pure generation, where no hardware exists at all and a model simply imagined the scene frame by frame. That last category is the one to watch. We've entered an age where a convincing product reveal costs less than a decent lunch, and anyone can show you a future that has no factory behind it. So build a habit. Ask three questions before you believe a robot clip: Was it one continuous take? Does the environment change when the camera moves? Can anyone actually buy it? If the answers are no, no, and "waitlist," enjoy it as a vision, not as evidence. And here's the twist: it doesn't matter as much as you'd think. The vision is the thing being sold. The expectation it plants will shape what gets built, funded, and bought for the next ten years. Fiction has always led engineering. Star Trek gave us the flip phone. Neuromancer gave us the word cyberspace. A rendered kitchen may give us the household robot. ──────────── 18. THE FOUR STAGES EVERY NEW TECHNOLOGY GOES THROUGH I've watched this pattern across mobile, social, crypto, and AI. It repeats with unsettling reliability. Stage one: Ridicule. "That's a toy. It'll never replace the real thing." Stage two: Fear. "It will destroy jobs, relationships, civilization." Stage three: Quiet adoption. People use it privately while criticizing it publicly. Stage four: Invisibility. Nobody talks about it. It's just how things are. Home robots are currently somewhere between stage one and stage two. The reaction to every clip will be a mix of mockery and dread, usually from the same people in the same thread. You can use this. When the reaction is loudest, adoption is closest. Noise is a leading indicator. ──────────── 19. THE JOBS THAT GET MORE VALUABLE, NOT LESS If you're nervous, good. Use the nerves. Skills that appreciate in a world of capable machines are the ones that machines are structurally bad at: Taste. Deciding what's worth doing at all. Trust. Being the person someone chooses to believe. Judgment under ambiguity, when the rules run out. Physical presence in situations where humanity is the point: births, deaths, apologies, weddings. Teaching, in the real sense: shaping how someone thinks, not what they memorize. Craft that carries a name: the thing people value precisely because a specific person made it. Notice the pattern. Every item involves meaning, not output. When production gets cheap, meaning gets expensive. ──────────── 20. WHAT I WOULD DO IF I HAD TWELVE MONTHS Practical section. Save this one. Learn to work with machines, not against them. Not coding necessarily. Direction. Specifying what you want clearly, checking the result, catching the subtle mistake. The people who manage fleets of agents and robots will out-earn the people who compete with them. Own something physical and local. A service business, a workshop, a clinic, a restaurant. Software gets copied overnight. A trusted name on a real street corner doesn't. Build an audience. Not for vanity. For distribution. In a world of infinite generated content, the scarce asset is a person other people already trust. Protect your attention like a bank account. The next decade will be engineered, with extraordinary skill, to take it from you. Keep one skill that is stubbornly, visibly human. Cooking for friends. Playing an instrument badly in public. Teaching a kid to ride a bike. Things worth doing precisely because a machine could do them better. ──────────── 21. THE QUESTION UNDERNEATH ALL OF THIS Strip away the hardware, the funding rounds, the regulations, the hot takes. One question is left, and it has been left for every generation that got a new power: When you're freed from the thing you were forced to do, who do you choose to become? The washing machine freed generations of women from a full day of laundry. Some of that time became careers. Some became rest. Some became new burdens, because expectations rose to fill the space. Freedom from necessity has never automatically produced freedom for something. That part has always been up to us. If a machine hands you back a thousand hours a year, the real product isn't the machine. It's the thousand hours. And the real question isn't what it can do for you. It's what you'll do with the time. ──────────── 22. MY HONEST TAKE, WITHOUT THE HEDGING I think this wave is real. I think it arrives faster than institutions are prepared for and slower than the loudest accounts claim, which is the usual pattern. I think the first decade will be messy, uneven, and expensive, with a few genuinely terrible stories and a few genuinely beautiful ones. I think the households that adopt thoughtfully, with boundaries, local data, and clear rules about what the machine does and doesn't do, will gain something rare: time that belongs to them. And I think the households that adopt carelessly will discover, a little too late, that a perfectly obedient servant can quietly rewrite the family around it. Technology doesn't decide which of those futures you get. Defaults do. Pricing does. Regulation does. Habits do. And so does the one thing that has never been automated: deciding what you refuse to outsource. ──────────── 23. A FIVE-SECOND STRESS TEST FOR ANY ROBOT COMPANY Before you trust a startup with a machine that lives in your house, ask: Where does my data go, and can I turn that off? What happens to the robot if your company shuts down next year? Can it work with the internet unplugged? Who can see through its cameras, and is that logged somewhere I can read? What does the physical emergency stop look like? If the answers are vague, the price is not the real cost. ──────────── 24. THE ONLY SENTENCE YOU NEED TO REMEMBER Every era gets a machine that changes what ordinary life feels like. Ours has a tall cardboard box, a tracking number, and a glass of juice. The question was never whether it would arrive. It's whether you'll be ready when it knocks. ──────────── If this made you think, do three things: Follow for more breakdowns of where technology is actually heading, before it hits the front page.
